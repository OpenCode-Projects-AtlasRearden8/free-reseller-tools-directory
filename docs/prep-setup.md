# The Old-School Prep Setup

*A plain-English guide to getting items from "the pile" to "listed and shipped" — with paper, bins, and one good photo spot. No fancy software required.*

Written by Asdale Dynamics, LLC. Plain English, step by step, never condescending.

---

## Why old-school still wins

Apps are great, but every thriving reseller we know runs the same spine underneath the software: **a place for things to land, a way to know what's what, and a habit of doing the boring part on the same day every week.** This guide sets up that spine with physical supplies, a notebook, and (optionally) the free tools in our directory (`site/index.html`).

You need about $60 in supplies the first time and about zero dollars a month after.

## The core loop

Everything you do fits one of five stations:

1. **Intake** — stuff comes in the door and waits in one spot.
2. **Prep** — clean it, test it, photograph it, bag it.
3. **Logging** — write down what it is, what you paid (even if it's "$0 — my closet").
4. **Listing** — put it online with a keyword-checked title (see the directory's Keyword Research section).
5. **Pack & ship** — sold? Weigh it, check a rate (directory: Shipping Calculators), pack, label, send.

Keep the stations physically separate, even if "separate" is four corners of a spare room. Mixing them is how death piles happen.

## The shelf and the bins

Buy three bin sizes at a dollar store or use decent moving boxes:

- **Intake bin** — unsorted. It's allowed to exist. It's not allowed to be the whole system.
- **To-list bin** — prepped, photographed, logged, waiting to be listed.
- **Sold shelf** — anything with a SKU on it that's paid for, arranged newest-payout-first.

**SKU system (this is the whole trick):** when an item is logged, write a short code on masking tape or a return address label and stick it on the item or its bag: `26-A-014` means year 2026, source A (see your log), item 14. You do not need to remember what anything is. The SKU finds it for you.

## The photo spot (a $0 booth)

A rolling cart or a corner of a table is fine:

1. One white or off-white poster board as a sweep: tape the top to a wall, curl the bottom onto the table. Now your background disappears.
2. Light from a bright window from the side (not behind your item). Add one cheap clamp light if nights are your photo time.
3. Your phone on a stand or a stack of books. Wipe the lens first; it changes everything.
4. Shoot: front, back, both sides, any flaw close-up, plus a size reference (mannequin, hanger, ruler, hand).

The free editors in the directory (Photopea, GIMP) handle crop, brighten, and cleanup. That's 80% of listing-photo quality — the other 20% was shooting in decent light.

## The one-tab ledger

The old school way is a notebook; the modern-free way is one Google Sheets tab (directory: Sales Trackers). Columns, in order:

| Column | Why |
|---|---|
| SKU | Your `26-A-014` code |
| Item & brand | "Levi's 511 jeans, 32×30, used" |
| Source | "Garage sale, Mrs. Kim" or "Storage unit 214" |
| Cost | Even $0 — honest numbers make honest profit |
| Listed price | Not the profit; just what you asked |
| Date sold | The heartbeat of the business |
| Sold price | What actually landed |
| Fees + shipping | Round up; surprises live here |
| Profit | This column is why you're doing this |

At the end of each week, add one summary row: totals for the week. Nothing fancy — just so the ledger tells the story at a glance instead of making you scroll a hundred entries.

## Prepping different kinds of items

**Clothing:** lint roller, cut loose threads, tie tags with painter's tape (never pins through fabric), fold to a standard size (juniors' book or cutting mat for guidance), and poly-bag anything with prints, fuzz, or glitter.

**Hard goods:** blast dust with canned air or a clean dry paintbrush, test batteries (fresh ones, and say so in the listing), photograph working screens and ports, note missing parts honestly and price accordingly.

**Smalls:** small parts in zip bags taped to the main item, labeled with the same SKU. The #1 way to lose money on a sale is shipping an un-bagged screw set.

**Media & games:** capture flaws at an angle (scratches read better in raking light), keep discs in the sleeve you photographed, photograph the exact sleeve and disc together with the SKU note.

## Pack-and-ship without drama

1. **Choose weight-saving over sturdiness only when items are unbreakable.** Books ship media-style; glass gets bubble wrap, double-boxing, and a sturdier rate.
2. **Weigh on a kitchen or dollar-store scale while the box is flat-empty,** then add measured packing materials — don't guess high and pay for phantom pounds.
3. **Check rates before you promise a price in the listing.** Pirate Ship compares USPS and UPS in one screen; the USPS calculator confirms flat-rate box math (both in the directory).
4. **Keep three box sizes on hand** and reuse clean, strong boxes with labels fully covered.
5. **Tape the SKU on the outside too** (or write the buyer's Marketplace order ID on it) — future-you, holding twenty orders at once, will be grateful.

## Weekly rhythm (low-energy friendly)

Pick one day a week and do the whole loop in one pass, or split it: photos on Saturday, listing on Sunday, packing each evening whenever an order lands. ADHD-friendly versions of this list: set a 25-minute timer per station, and let the timer end the session. A little every week beats a heroic weekend you never have again.

A batch-week looks like:

- **Monday:** intake bin empty-ish, everything cleaned and faded-out checked.
- **Tuesday:** photo booth open, everything shot in one sitting.
- **Wednesday:** write listings in batches of ten with keyword research open.
- **Daily, five minutes:** sold? pack it today. Even tomorrow is late.

## Paper trail that saves you at tax time

Keep an accordion folder, one slot per month. Receipts for supplies, postage (Pirate Ship keeps digital receipts automatically — export them monthly into the folder or a cloud drive), sourcing mileage (notebook in the car), and your ledger's month-end totals. When a 1099-K shows up in January, you'll open a folder, not a fire. This is general information, not tax advice — for your specific situation, ask a tax professional.
